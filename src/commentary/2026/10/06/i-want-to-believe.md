In the abstract, I tend to agree with this part’s overarching wish:  The Internet *should be* open—and it *should be* “incomplete” per Komaitis’ nomenclature, though I’d prefer different nomenclature.

The difference is not insignificant.  For comparison, art as a whole is never “complete” in that sense—yet many artists are guided by a grand vision of what art should be, and how people should do it.  Excepting those who are of some modern and postmodern movements, or those who otherwise focus on the pathos of the human condition, I don’t think any artist who slaves away at a work (only to have it plagiarized by an LLM forger) would idealize artistic incompleteness as a guiding vision.

(The exceptions, of course, are the artists who declare that *art sucks*,[^sucks] its truths are false, or, as did Henry Miller in the very modern *Tropic of Cancer*, “I have no money, no resources, no hopes.  \[...\]  This is not a book.  This is libel, slander, defamation of character.  \[...\] \[T\]his is a prolonged insult, a gob of spit in the face of Art, a kick in the pants to God, Man, Destiny, Time, Love, Beauty … \[...\] I will dance over your dirty corpse…”)

[^sucks]: Political correction:  The negative cultural colloquialism is hereby referenced without offense to porn stars and other people who suck, as do I, *impurify*.

Perforce, that is a rough analogy.  It more reflects how, although I want to believe in where this essay goes, I doubt that it gets there.

> The internet belonged to this family of unfinished systems.  This is perhaps the most misunderstood aspect of its design.  We often describe the internet as open, decentralized or interoperable, and while each of these descriptions is accurate, they are ultimately consequences of a more fundamental characteristic.  The internet was deliberately incomplete.  Its architects resisted the temptation to determine in advance how people should use it because they recognized that no one could anticipate the forms of innovation that would emerge decades later.

Although there have always been people with such opinions, I think that’s overall an historically inaccurate assessment.  For one of many counterexamples that I could cite, *see* an RFC that [I referenced off-hand](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/10/01/the-metric-is-not-the-mission-the-digital-climate/#comment-5602970) in the dumpster fire of the previous thread:  The Internet Architecture Board, J. Kempf and R. Austein (eds.) (2004), [RFC 3724, “The Rise of the Middle and the Future of End-to-End: Reflections on the Evolution of the Internet Architecture”](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc3724.html).

I invoked RFC 3724, because I personally find it a kind of an abstruse touchstone.  In the staid tones of professional Internet Architects,[^slap] it reads to me like screaming, **YOU’RE DOING IT ALL WRONG!!!11**  Whereas the term “end-to-end” as used in RFC 3724 is not even understood by most technical people nowadays.  Although it’s only 22 years old, this RFC seems ancient—and although it’s *only* 22 years old, it reaches back into the architectural history of the proto-Internet, going back to ARPANET.

[^slap]: *Cf.* some academic research scientists whom I saw critique junk science as “data-poor”.  *Ouch.*  Compare also judges who can bench-slap a party (or another judge) in such cold legal language, it burns by the ice of its understatement.

But it is only exemplary.  My point is that Internet architects tend to have definite opinions on *how the Net should work*.  It’s their job, after all.

Anodyne ideas about how we don’t know the system’s future may feel good, but they do little to define the protocols for a functioning internetwork.  For that, a prescriptive vision is required—even some authoritarian judgment.  Per my example, the bygone “end-to-end” principle is an architecture for the freedom of netizens—but the Net’s builders, it is a severe constraint, nigh architectural bondage-and-discipline, the oath of people who cut their teeth building a network contrary to the telcos’ architecture.

I think that many early Net pioneers had sharp opinions “in advance \[about\] how people should use” the Net.  They had influence, too.  However, they were out-influenced by money and by the discourses of power.

> The platforms stopped behaving as participants within a broader ecosystem and began organizing the ecosystem around themselves.  Their success no longer depended primarily on helping users explore the web; increasingly, it depended on reducing the need to leave the platform at all.  \[...\]
>
> None of this represented a conspiracy against the open internet.  It emerged from incentives that were, from the perspective of individual firms, entirely rational.

A minor point:  Rational behavior does not exclude a conspiracy against the Net’s openness.  To the contrary, rational actors who seek power tend rationally to seek more power—and if one wants to call it by that loaded word, a “conspiracy” is an excellent means to achieve that.  Moreover, sociopaths can be rational; indeed, an economist must call a sociopath the perfect *economically rational actor*, who is game-theoretically optimal for capitalism.[^musk]

[^musk]: By the metric of capital net worth, Elon Musk is also game-theoretically optimal for capitalism.  However, I have some doubts about whether Elon is a sociopath; he seems too much given to delusional flights of fancy.

Beyond that, there is copious scholarly evidence, including from authors whom this series has previously cited (such as Shoshana Zuboff), for intentional designs and cold calculations to attain goals so nearly indistinguishable from a conspiracy to close the Net and lock in users, to attempt to separate the concepts would be to split hairs.

So as for the high-level question of what the Net should *be*—and of whose interests it should serve.  On a low-level, fundamental level, the most basic conditions for an open Internet have been systematically, intentionally attacked.  To support this assertion, I could give many examples—but I need here state only one.

The Apple-Google duopoly has demolished commonplace ownership and usage of *unrestricted, general-purpose personal computers*.  On a deep level, their “smartphone” platforms with “app stores” are an *architectural* attack on the open Internet.  And methinks we can take judicial notice of the fact that their campaign to universalize these “devices” has been well-organized and systematic.

They have far more practical control of your phone than the phone company did, way back when, [in America, AT&T legally owned the telephone in your home](https://dcchs.org/the-era-of-ma-bell/).  But now, a phone is much more than just a phone:  It’s most people’s primary, or even only point of Net access.  This is not a matter of “emergent” behaviors by unthinking actors:  **It’s a “conspiracy” for the telcos to usurp the Net that usurped them.**[^phone-numbers]

[^phone-numbers]: *Cf.* my comments on several prior parts of this series, as to phone numbers which were supposed to be obsoleted by the Net.

> The web did not emerge from the telecommunications monopolies.

No, but it’s now all but owned as a corporate asset by Ma Bell’s conceptual successors.  (And by the way...

> Open-source software challenged assumptions held by the largest software companies of its time.

Ever since Microsoft discovered that Github could be used to *embrace-and-extend* a development paradigm, “open source” has become a scam for corporations to extract free labor from nerds whose hearts are even bigger than their brains.  And that was before Copilot!)

Again, that is only one example.  It must needs suffice—lest I add another thousand words barely scratching the surface of how the so-called “AI” companies have systematically strip-mined the Net, and by conscious intention designed to turn the Net into a catch-net for human slaves ruled by robots.

———

In its conclusion, this essay offers much hope.  I’d like to believe that the Net is more “permanent” than its usurpers.  But that is not an historical inevitability.  I fear that such a line of thought could attenuate the drive for community organizing and radical action to push people off of the corporate-silo platforms, to get *general-purpose personal computers* back into the hands of the masses, and most of all, to annihilate the “AI” companies with extreme prejudice.

We need to understand that this is not a matter of well-meaning people blindly following wherever capitalist market incentives may lead them:  It is a war of visions, not only for how the Net should work, but ultimately for how the world should be governed.
