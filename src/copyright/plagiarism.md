<span>Let’s talk</span> about Shakespeare.

The complete works of William Shakespeare lack so-called “copyright protection”.  Shakespeare is in the public domain—*uncopyrighted*.  Note that Shakespeare didn’t use a <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/"><abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> copyright license</a>, which is anyway inapplicable to works written four centuries ago.  Does all of this mean that you can copy Shakespeare without attribution, and even rip him off as if you wrote his work?

Of course not!  Yet the proper attribution of Shakespeare is an issue which, in itself, has *nothing whatsoever* to do with copyright.  Only a profound confusion of concepts would cause anyone to assume that Shakespeare needs <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> to get credit for his work, and to stop people from *plagiarizing* him.

Despite the obviousness[^nonobvious] of my *reductio ad absurdum* about Shakespeare, that same confusion is endemic about and amongst living and recently-deceased authors, artists, musicians, and <abbr title="Free and Open-Source Software">FOSS</abbr> programmers.

[^nonobvious]: My declaration of “obviousness” is rhetorical:  It’s obvious *to me*, but I’ve never seen anyone else make this argument.  Thus, it is original of (or at least, independent by) me, and perforce objectively nonobvious.

## Confusing similar, irreconcilably different concepts. { #similar-different }

In modern law, copyright is *only and exclusively* about *money*.  It is a regime of rent extraction, an artificial monopoly status that creates landlords of the mind.  Its benefits accrue primarily to publishers—not only severally from, but to the detriment of authors who are inured to the promotion of others’ interests by the mirage of defending their own.  The poison fruits of copyright are accordingly offensive to intellectuals,[^custodians] to many programmers,[^unlicense] to not a few artists,[^artnocopyright] to many classical musicians,[^imslp] and to popular musicians who are not sellouts.[^trentfucksgreedyassholes]  Creative spirits who reject copyright law may resort to enforcement of extralegal norms via social shaming,[^intotheirownhands] and even *attempting* to find noncopyright means to attain digital market scarcity.[^authenticity]

Historically, the antecedents of copyright were even worse:  English copyright law grew out of religious censorship laws[^copyhistory] from the era of the European Wars of Religion.  In substantial effect, the Crown deputized private parties, the licensed owners of printing presses, as moral-political wardens to suppress heresy and sedition—for in a state with an established church, the two are one and the same.  By the eternal Law of Unintended Consequences, by the time that the laws lost their original purpose, they had created an entrenched lobby of economic interests vested in a novel, wholly artificial “right” to *a monopoly on* copying.

[^copyhistory]:
    I can herewith only but summarize an enormously complex topic that deserves its own treatment at length.

    For a brief conspectus on the origin of the concept of an exclusive right to make copies, *see* L. Ray Patterson (1993), <a href="https://digitalcommons.law.uga.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1342&context=fac_artchop" type="application/pdf">“Copyright and ‘the Exclusive Right’ of Authors”</a>, *J. Intell. Prop. L.* **(1)**1: 1–48, esp. at 8–14.  For greater historical depth from the same author, *see* Lyman Ray Patterson (1968), *Copyright in Historical Perspective*, Vanderbilt University Press: Nashville, Tennessee (shadow library md5: `3d381cf7c6324a296eb076cf517db20e`); an overview at p. 6 places censorship and copyright in the context of a brief outline of the book, and Ch.&nbsp; 6 at 114–142, “Copyright and Censorship”, covers the historical relation of copyright to English censorship in greater detail.

    Patterson’s view of historical copyright and censorship is tempered—indeed, moreso than seems to be represented by some anticopyright activists whom I’ve seen cite his 1993 paper.  Nonetheless, he does strongly link the development of English copyright law to censorship “\[d\]uring almost the whole of the period from 1557 to 1709, a time of continuous religious struggle, \[when\] censorship was a major policy of the English government” (Patterson 1968 at 6).  He finds in particular that “\[e\]xcept for censorship, the courts might well have imposed limitations on the early copyright to preclude its use as an instrument of monopoly; but the presence of censorship protected copyright from judicial interference” (*id.* at 114).  I think it’s clear that absent its historical entwining with religious censorship, copyright could not have developed in anything even remotely resembling its current form.  In particular, at the time when, in the Eighteenth Century, the entrenched stationers and booksellers turned disingenuously to arguing for an author’s right “to turn a legal question into a political question” (Patterson 1993, p. 13) and thus protect their own monopoly positions, I observe that they would have had neither the legal structure, nor the lobbying influence, nor the financial and organizational strength of, in effect, a powerful cartel.

    Upon this and other sources, I have been developing an historiographic thesis, *The Gutenberg Principle* (unpublished), which sets this history in a much broader perspective.

*Plagiary* is an etymological metaphor for violence:  It is literally *kidnapping*.  It is the crime of pretenders who create not, at the expense of creators.  Accordingly, it is long reprehended, and nigh universally so amongst thinkers and artists.  Plagiarism is a fraud most odious to the mind, which has been condemned by intellectuals for thousands of years.

Copyright is motivated by greed.  The moral against plagiarism is a matter of integrity.

Only a cheap sell-out loves copyright, but only a rip-off charlatan excuses plagiarism.  Of course, the two are not mutually exclusive:  A plagiarist may be motivated by greed to extract rents on copyrighted plagiarism.

Copyright strangles culture.  Plagiarism poisons culture.

Albeit not always, the copyright industry so oft conflates these issues.  Alas, so does <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr>.

## The discursive mind-trap of the copyright dialectic. { #copyright-dialectic }

I’m reluctant to criticize <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr>.  Creative Commons is generally run by good people, and they’ve done much good.  The Creative Commons Attribution license is one of their greatest successes.  And my reluctance to criticize it is a part of the problem.

If one ignores the forest for the trees, <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> is a cleverly pragmatic law-hack.  Via copyright law, <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> provides an author with a clear legal cause of action, and thus with effective recourse, against plagiarists.  For such reasons, even I may sometimes use it—perhaps.  Otherwise, a plagiarized free-culture author may have difficulty seeking relief in the courts...

...and that difficulty, in itself, is an ill caused by the *social dialectic*[^social-dialectic] of copyright law, which, per the same dialectic, is presented as a cure.

Plagiarism is typically punished by institutions and by individuals, by *de facto* private law systems (*e.g.,* university codes) and by consensus customs.  A plagiarist shall be expelled from universities, fired from jobs, blacklisted by publishers, and shamed and shunned by everyone with even an ounce of sense.  But what *laws* has a plagiarist violated?  And in a court of law, how could damages, which may in many cases be largely intangible, be calculated *and adequately proved* without the statutory damages for copyright infringement?

I suppose that perhaps, in extreme cases, a plagiarist may be civilly and/or criminally liable per the five classic elements of fraud in common-law jurisdictions:  (a) Misrepresentations of (b) material fact, made with (c) intent to defraud, upon which others (d) reasonably relied, to their (e) detriment.  Tort lawyers may feel free to pipe up with fancier ideas, and perhaps even cases.  (<abbr title="I am not a lawyer">IANAL</abbr>.)  But there is no statutory cause of action for plagiarism like that which the U.S. Congress has provided for copyright infringement—and even the U.S. Constitution suffers this disparity in its grants of authority to Congress, or lack thereof.  Although perhaps, other jurisdictions moral rights laws *may* sometimes provide recourse (a statement I make as a question rather than an answer), there is no global plagiarism law treaty like the Berne Convention.

I hereby propose a new amendment to the U.S. Constitution, which (a) repeals Congress’ Copyright Clause[^copyrightclause] authority to legislate copyright, and (b) instead authorizes Congress to legislate against plagiarism.  Congress should then pass national plagiarism laws similar to current copyright law, with a statutory cause of action and statutory damages.  Under this new constitutional regime, *copying* is irrelevant; only *attribution* and thus also, intentional or knowing misattribution of a fraudulent nature[^notnegligence] are within the ambit of the law.

[^copyrightclause]: U.S. Const. [art. I, § 8](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States_of_America#Section_8), cl. 8 (in pertinent part).

[^notnegligence]:
    I hesitate to admit negligence (or even recklessness) as culpability for legally actionable plagiarism.  Although I may reconsider this, the potential for abuse of the law seems too high.  If the plaintiff or complainant bears the burden of proving intentionality or knowingness to the requisite standard of evidence, that seems a high enough bar to deter the worst foreseeable abuses in a litigious society.

    Otherwise stated:  Although I may yell at them *(assholes!)*, I don’t want to open the floodgates of legal liability for kids who thoughtlessly pass around fun images and bits of text without proper attribution.

    Moreover, at the boundaries, the edge cases are too many, and grey areas too large.  For instance, without pretense that I myself created a work I didn’t, I myself sometimes omit attributions when I reasonably guess that the creator may prefer not to have them for controversial or socially stigmatized usages.  This could be the start of a long list of edge cases.  Laws never handle edge cases optimally.

   Overall, I think it’s wisest to cabin my hypothetical plagiarism law by deriving its concepts from the law of fraud, and setting a reasonably high culpability standard.  When a person who copies and pastes sentences, paragraphs, or whole essays misattributed to themselves, they’re acting with fraudulent motives.  *Mutatis mutandis*, so, too, is a person who uses LLM slop to make themselves sound smart—and so, too, are LLM operators, on a mass scale.

Why, to my knowledge, am I the first person in the world ever to make this proposal, or even anything like it?  Uncharitably but not unjustly, one may blame “the <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> discourse”.

Creators who avail themselves of <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr>’s reliefs are thus locked into a discursive box.  Their discourse about plagiarism not only confuses it with copyright infringement, but *erases* the former, and *replaces* it with the latter.  The accusation, “You plagiarized me,” becomes, “You violated my copyright license, and you thus infringed my copyright with illegal copies outside of my legal monopoly on copying.”  They rarely, if ever pause to consider that it is *not okay* to plagiarize the public domain works of Shakespeare.

This is only exemplary of the wider discursive problem—and it’s such an extreme case, it is practically a *reductio ad absurdum*.  <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> users are by definition not the greatest fans of copyright.  If they can be discursively conformed to copyright fallacies, what are the prospects for copyright’s true-believers?

## In re the World v. OpenAI, Anthropic, et al. { #world-v-ai }

*When your only tool is a hammer, every problem looks like a nail.*  —That’s a Foucaudian critique of the discursive production of really dumb hammers, straight from the most pomo regions of the Ivory Tower.

A few years ago, some various people started to sue the *soi-disant* “artificial intelligence” companies for copyright infringement.  Privately, my first knee-jerk reaction was anger at the lawsuits.  After all, I hate copyright—frankly *hate it*.  As I have just spent so many words saying, I advocate that copyright law should be abolished altogether.

But then, I thought twice about it—and even thrice.  My ultimate conclusion is in agreement with [my reading of Bruce Schneier and J. B. Branch’s January 2026 opinion](../aaron-swartz/techdirt-suppressed-ai-discussion/impurify-textual-criticism.html)—modulo the problem that all that the law, and the discourse of the law gives us is a bag of broken hammers.

## “AI” consists of mass-plagiarism machines. { #ai-mass-plagiarism }

Let’s return to the start hereof.

The complete works of Shakespeare are in the public domain.  *Uncopyrighted.*  Is it okay to take one of Shakespeare’s sonnets, put your name on it, and republish it as your own work?  Of course not!

Well, what if you change a few words here and there?  Or mix and match passages from a few of Shakespeare’s sonnets?  These are classic methods of *cheating*, which will get you expelled from a university or will incinerate your career in a scandal when you are caught.

—Well, what if you feed all public-domain sonnets ever published in the English language into a machine that mixes them up for you into a statistical predictor that extrudes a sonnet-shaped glob of text?  That’s the Bender, *et al.* *stochastic parrot*[^stochasticparrots] or the Bender and Hanna *synthetic text extruding machine*,[^textextruder] which hype-mongers dishonestly sell to an ignorant public as “artificial intelligence”.

## We need radical new cultural discourse.

To that end, I hope hereby to plant the seed of an idea.

In the abstract, fundamentally, we need a cultural discourse of respect for creators—*wholly divorced from the discourse of copyright*.  I start the discourse at plagiarism, based not only on abstract principles, but also on a reality that experience suggests:  *Many* creative types of people care little, or not at all, about copyright—while almost every creative person is outraged to see others claim credit for their work.

Outcomes will logically include the discursive production of material benefits for creatives.  In a culture that values creativity and its authenticity,[^authenticity1] people of adequate means are not inclined to enjoy the fruits of creative work without giving anything back.  From the history of aristocratic patronage long antedating copyright, to pop-culture fans who buy all the <abbr title="merchandise">merch</abbr> they can get their hands on, a healthy cultural response to creative work is, “Shut up and take my money!”[^shut-up-and-take-my-money]  It is a response *harmed* by the discourse of copyright, which muddies clear waters with rent-seeking by oppressive monopolists.

I mention that in passing—necessarily, but briefly.  This is not, and cannot be a discourse driven by money.  It is a discourse for, of, and about creatives who, if necessary, would work “dayjobs” they hate to fund the work they love.

Colloquially, the discourse starts here:  *Hey, did you find a cool photo or graphic you want to pass around?  Please at least* try *to discover who made it, and give them credit when you show it to your friends.*

In the discourse of law, it starts by freeing the spirit of <abbr title="Creative Commons Attribution">CC-BY</abbr> from the imprisoning letter of copyright.

And overall, we must start by reminding everyone that in a culture expressed by texts, images, and sounds, it’s *never* okay to do, or even to tolerate the plagiaristic pretenses and personal frauds that would get someone expelled from school or fired from a job.  *That includes “AI” slop.*
