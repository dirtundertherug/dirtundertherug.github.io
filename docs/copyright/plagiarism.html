Copyright versus Plagiarism

Know the difference for a freer culture, a better public discourse—and a more effective fight against “AI”.

By impurify at dirtundertherug.com (LibrePGP 0x666BE6DF7F1093CD).
Published: 2026-09-23
Version: 0.9.0

Let’s talk about Shakespeare.

The complete works of William Shakespeare lack so-called “copyright protection”. Shakespeare is in the public domain—uncopyrighted. Note that Shakespeare didn’t use a CC-BY copyright license, which is anyway inapplicable to works written four centuries ago. Does all of this mean that you can copy Shakespeare without attribution, and even rip him off as if you wrote his work?

Of course not! Yet the proper attribution of Shakespeare is an issue which, in itself, has nothing whatsoever to do with copyright. Only a profound confusion of concepts would cause anyone to assume that Shakespeare needs CC-BY to get credit for his work, and to stop people from plagiarizing him.

Despite the obviousness1 of my reductio ad absurdum about Shakespeare, that same confusion is endemic about and amongst living and recently-deceased authors, artists, musicians, and FOSS programmers.

Confusing similar, irreconcilably different concepts.

In modern law, copyright is only and exclusively about money. It is a regime of rent extraction, an artificial monopoly status that creates landlords of the mind. Its benefits accrue primarily to publishers—not only severally from, but to the detriment of authors who are inured to the promotion of others’ interests by the mirage of defending their own. The poison fruits of copyright are accordingly offensive to intellectuals,2 to many programmers,3 to not a few artists,4 to many classical musicians,5 and to popular musicians who are not sellouts.6 Creative spirits who reject copyright law may resort to enforcement of extralegal norms via social shaming,7 and even attempting to find noncopyright means to attain digital market scarcity.8

Historically, the antecedents of copyright were even worse: English copyright law grew out of religious censorship laws9 from the era of the European Wars of Religion. In substantial effect, the Crown deputized private parties, the licensed owners of printing presses, as moral-political wardens to suppress heresy and sedition—for in a state with an established church, the two are one and the same. By the eternal Law of Unintended Consequences, by the time that the laws lost their original purpose, they had created an entrenched lobby of economic interests vested in a novel, wholly artificial “right” to a monopoly on copying.

Plagiary is an etymological metaphor for violence: It is literally kidnapping. It is the crime of pretenders who create not, at the expense of creators. Accordingly, it is long reprehended, and nigh universally so amongst thinkers and artists. Plagiarism is a fraud most odious to the mind, which has been condemned by intellectuals for thousands of years.

Copyright is motivated by greed. The moral against plagiarism is a matter of integrity.

Only a cheap sell-out loves copyright, but only a rip-off charlatan excuses plagiarism. Of course, the two are not mutually exclusive: A plagiarist may be motivated by greed to extract rents on copyrighted plagiarism.

Copyright strangles culture. Plagiarism poisons culture.

Albeit not always, the copyright industry so oft conflates these issues. Alas, so does CC-BY.

The discursive mind-trap of the copyright dialectic.

I’m reluctant to criticize CC-BY. Creative Commons is generally run by good people, and they’ve done much good. The Creative Commons Attribution license is one of their greatest successes. And my reluctance to criticize it is a part of the problem.

If one ignores the forest for the trees, CC-BY is a cleverly pragmatic law-hack. Via copyright law, CC-BY provides an author with a clear legal cause of action, and thus with effective recourse, against plagiarists. For such reasons, even I may sometimes use it—perhaps. Otherwise, a plagiarized free-culture author may have difficulty seeking relief in the courts...

...and that difficulty, in itself, is an ill caused by the social dialectic10 of copyright law, which, per the same dialectic, is presented as a cure.

Plagiarism is typically punished by institutions and by individuals, by de facto private law systems (e.g., university codes) and by consensus customs. A plagiarist shall be expelled from universities, fired from jobs, blacklisted by publishers, and shamed and shunned by everyone with even an ounce of sense. But what laws has a plagiarist violated? And in a court of law, how could damages, which may in many cases be largely intangible, be calculated and adequately proved without the statutory damages for copyright infringement?

I suppose that perhaps, in extreme cases, a plagiarist may be civilly and/or criminally liable per the five classic elements of fraud in common-law jurisdictions: (a) Misrepresentations of (b) material fact, made with (c) intent to defraud, upon which others (d) reasonably relied, to their (e) detriment. Tort lawyers may feel free to pipe up with fancier ideas, and perhaps even cases. (IANAL.) But there is no statutory cause of action for plagiarism like that which the U.S. Congress has provided for copyright infringement—and even the U.S. Constitution suffers this disparity in its grants of authority to Congress, or lack thereof. Although perhaps, other jurisdictions moral rights laws may sometimes provide recourse (a statement I make as a question rather than an answer), there is no global plagiarism law treaty like the Berne Convention.

I hereby propose a new amendment to the U.S. Constitution, which (a) repeals Congress’ Copyright Clause11 authority to legislate copyright, and (b) instead authorizes Congress to legislate against plagiarism. Congress should then pass national plagiarism laws similar to current copyright law, with a statutory cause of action and statutory damages. Under this new constitutional regime, copying is irrelevant; only attribution and thus also, intentional or knowing misattribution of a fraudulent nature12 are within the ambit of the law.

Why, to my knowledge, am I the first person in the world ever to make this proposal, or even anything like it? Uncharitably but not unjustly, one may blame “the CC-BY discourse”.

Creators who avail themselves of CC-BY’s reliefs are thus locked into a discursive box. Their discourse about plagiarism not only confuses it with copyright infringement, but erases the former, and replaces it with the latter. The accusation, “You plagiarized me,” becomes, “You violated my copyright license, and you thus infringed my copyright with illegal copies outside of my legal monopoly on copying.” They rarely, if ever pause to consider that it is not okay to plagiarize the public domain works of Shakespeare.

This is only exemplary of the wider discursive problem—and it’s such an extreme case, it is practically a reductio ad absurdum. CC-BY users are by definition not the greatest fans of copyright. If they can be discursively conformed to copyright fallacies, what are the prospects for copyright’s true-believers?

In re the World v. OpenAI, Anthropic, et al.

When your only tool is a hammer, every problem looks like a nail. —That’s a Foucaudian critique of the discursive production of really dumb hammers, straight from the most pomo regions of the Ivory Tower.

A few years ago, some various people started to sue the soi-disant “artificial intelligence” companies for copyright infringement. Privately, my first knee-jerk reaction was anger at the lawsuits. After all, I hate copyright—frankly hate it. As I have just spent so many words saying, I advocate that copyright law should be abolished altogether.

But then, I thought twice about it—and even thrice. My ultimate conclusion is in agreement with my reading of Bruce Schneier and J. B. Branch’s January 2026 opinion—modulo the problem that all that the law, and the discourse of the law gives us is a bag of broken hammers.

“AI” consists of mass-plagiarism machines.

Let’s return to the start hereof.

The complete works of Shakespeare are in the public domain. Uncopyrighted. Is it okay to take one of Shakespeare’s sonnets, put your name on it, and republish it as your own work? Of course not!

Well, what if you change a few words here and there? Or mix and match passages from a few of Shakespeare’s sonnets? These are classic methods of cheating, which will get you expelled from a university or will incinerate your career in a scandal when you are caught.

—Well, what if you feed all public-domain sonnets ever published in the English language into a machine that mixes them up for you into a statistical predictor that extrudes a sonnet-shaped glob of text? That’s the Bender, et al. stochastic parrot13 or the Bender and Hanna synthetic text extruding machine,14 which hype-mongers dishonestly sell to an ignorant public as “artificial intelligence”.

It cannot be copyright infringement: The LLM’s entire training dataset is in the public domain. But it’s wrong. It’s reprehensible. In the large, it is the end of human culture—and in the small, it’s a direct personal threat to every talented writer and artist.

Consider that hypothetical sonnet-parroting LLM. Suppose, quite plausibly, that it can produce writing which, to perhaps ninety-nine per cent of human readers, is indistinguishable from William Shakespeare. Thus does Shakespeare, no longer immortal, die of social grade inflation (my own original term for this). Shakespeare just isn’t special anymore, when anyone can use a mass-plagiarism machine to write (almost) just like Shakespeare.

Yet copyright law gives us grossly inadequate protection from this threat—and the discourse of copyright law constructs our thinking in the wrong terms. Half the debate about the copyright infringement suits (and worse, half the arguments of the plaintiffs) is all about how the “AI” companies must be made to pay licensing fees for their copying. Say what!?

On exigent grounds, with the only available tools we have, a case needs to be fought by someone who will never in any circumstance consent to the “AI” companies’ misappropriation of their works. Fuck money. This is not about money.

We need radical new cultural discourse.

To that end, I hope hereby to plant the seed of an idea.

In the abstract, fundamentally, we need a cultural discourse of respect for creators—wholly divorced from the discourse of copyright. I start the discourse at plagiarism, based not only on abstract principles, but also on a reality that experience suggests: Many creative types of people care little, or not at all, about copyright—while almost every creative person is outraged to see others claim credit for their work.

Outcomes will logically include the discursive production of material benefits for creatives. In a culture that values creativity and its authenticity,15 people of adequate means are not inclined to enjoy the fruits of creative work without giving anything back. From the history of aristocratic patronage long antedating copyright, to pop-culture fans who buy all the merch they can get their hands on, a healthy cultural response to creative work is, “Shut up and take my money!”16 It is a response harmed by the discourse of copyright, which muddies clear waters with rent-seeking by oppressive monopolists.

I mention that in passing—necessarily, but briefly. This is not, and cannot be a discourse driven by money. It is a discourse for, of, and about creatives who, if necessary, would work “dayjobs” they hate to fund the work they love.

Colloquially, the discourse starts here: Hey, did you find a cool photo or graphic you want to pass around? Please at least try to discover who made it, and give them credit when you show it to your friends.

In the discourse of law, it starts by freeing the spirit of CC-BY from the imprisoning letter of copyright.

And overall, we must start by reminding everyone that in a culture expressed by texts, images, and sounds, it’s never okay to do, or even to tolerate the plagiaristic pretenses and personal frauds that would get someone expelled from school or fired from a job. That includes “AI” slop.
